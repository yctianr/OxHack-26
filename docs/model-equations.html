Spillcast model equations

Every calculation behind the Spillcast website, in plain words, with the formula and the units of what it produces: the sewage works model and its 7-day forecast, the river water-quality model on the river page, the statistical backup and the regulator watchlist. Fitted and assumed values are listed at the end.

1 · The sewage works, hour by hour

The main model treats each works as plumbing. Water arrives from homes, hard surfaces, saturated ground and groundwater. The works treats up to its capacity, a storm tank holds the excess, and only what the tank can't hold spills to the river.

1.1

Sewage from people and businesses

A steady base flow, about 30% higher around midday and 30% lower overnight, because people use more water in the day. The base flow is fitted per works; across works it comes out at about 163 litres per person per day.

\[ \text{foul}_t = F\left(1 + 0.3\cos\frac{2\pi\,(h_t - 12)}{24}\right) \]
Unitm³ per hour
\(h_t\) is the local hour of the day.
1.2

How dry the soil is

The soil is a sponge that can take up to \(D_{max}\) mm of water. Rain fills it, evaporation dries it, more slowly as it gets drier. Rain that falls on a full sponge soaks down to the groundwater instead.

\[ D_t = D_{t-1} + \text{PET}_t\left(1 - \frac{D_{t-1}}{D_{max}}\right) - R_t \qquad \text{wetness}_t = 1 - \frac{D_t}{D_{max}} \]
Unitmm the soil could still absorb (0 = soaked)
\(R_t\) is rain and \(\text{PET}_t\) is evaporation in that hour, both in mm. If \(D_t\) would go below 0, the extra becomes groundwater recharge and \(D_t\) is set to 0.
1.3

Rain running into the sewers

Rain takes time to travel through the pipes, so each hour's rain is spread over the hours that follow. Two kinds of ground send it to the sewers: hard surfaces (roofs and roads) always drain, and permeable land drains only as the soil gets wet. One millimetre of rain on one hectare is 10 m³ of water.

\[ r_t = e^{-1/\tau_f}\, r_{t-1} + \left(1 - e^{-1/\tau_f}\right) R_t \] \[ \text{runoff}_t = 10 \times \left(A + A_s \cdot \text{wetness}_t\right) \times r_t \]
Unitm³ per hour
\(\tau_f\) is the sewer travel time in hours (1–12). \(A\) and \(A_s\) are areas in hectares.
1.4

Groundwater leaking into the pipes

Each works uses whichever of two versions fits its records better.

  • Groundwater store: rain that passed the full sponge fills an underground store that drains away over weeks. A fixed share of the store leaks into the pipes.
  • River-driven: leakage rises with the Thames flow at Farmoor above a threshold, using high river flow as a sign of high groundwater.
\[ G_t = e^{-1/(24\,\tau_g)}\, G_{t-1} + \text{recharge}_t \qquad \text{leakage}_t = k \cdot G_t \] \[ \text{or} \qquad \text{leakage}_t = k \cdot \max\!\left(0,\; Q^{\text{Farmoor}}_t - q_0\right) \]
Unitm³ per hour
\(\tau_g\) is the store's drain time in days (3–365). \(Q^{\text{Farmoor}}\) and \(q_0\) are in m³/s.
1.5

Total flow arriving at the works

The three sources add up. In the 200 uncertainty runs each day's total is multiplied by a random error \(m_t\); in the central run \(m_t = 1\).

\[ \text{inflow}_t = \left(\text{foul}_t + \text{leakage}_t + \text{runoff}_t\right) \times m_t \]
Unitm³ per hour
1.6

Treat, store or spill

  • Capacity \(C\) is the most the works treats in a storm: the level its daily treated flow reaches on its busiest 5% of days, divided by 24.
  • Below capacity: everything is treated, and the storm tank is pumped back for treatment.
  • Above capacity: the extra fills the storm tank, of volume \(V\) (from a published source, otherwise 2 hours at capacity).
  • Tank full: whatever still doesn't fit spills into the river.
\[ \text{treated}_t = \min(\text{inflow}_t,\, C) + \text{pumped back}_t \] \[ \text{spill}_t = \max\!\left(0,\; \text{inflow}_t - C - \text{tank space left}_t\right) \]
Unitm³ per hour (tank in m³)
1.7

How the model is tuned

The fitted numbers are adjusted until each day's modelled treated flow matches the treated volume the works reported (TDV). Spill records are never used. On days near capacity the record only says "at least this much arrived", so the model is corrected only if it predicts less.

\[ \text{error}_d = \log\Big(\sum_{t \in d} \text{treated}_t\Big) - \log\left(\text{TDV}_d\right) \] \[ \text{if } \text{TDV}_d \ge 0.9 \times \text{capacity}: \quad \text{error}_d = \min\!\Big(0,\; \log \sum_{t \in d} \text{inflow}_t - \log \text{TDV}_d\Big) \]
Unitnone (a log ratio; 0.1 ≈ 10% off)
1.8

From one run to a chance of spilling

The model runs 200 times with a realistic daily error on inflow. The error's size \(\sigma\) (typically 13%) and how much it carries over from day to day \(\rho\) both come from how far off the fit is on normal days.

\[ \varepsilon_d = \rho\,\varepsilon_{d-1} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\;\sigma\, z_d \qquad m_t = e^{\,\varepsilon_d - \sigma^2/2} \] \[ P(\text{spill on day } d) = \frac{\text{runs that spill that day}}{200} \]
Unitchance in %, spill-hours in hours, spill volume in m³
\(z_d\) is a random draw from a standard normal distribution. Spill-hours and volume are averages over the 200 runs.
1.9

The rain slider

On the scenario page the slider reruns one day for the selected works, starting from that morning's soil, groundwater and tank, with the day's hourly rain scaled to the new total. The published numbers move by the difference.

\[ P_{\text{shown}} = P_{\text{published}} + P_{\text{new rain}} - P_{\text{rain that fell}} \]
Unit%, and the same shift for hours and m³
1.10

The 7-day forecast

Each works carries on from the end of the observed data, starting from its real soil, groundwater, sewer and tank state. Rain comes from all 51 members of the ECMWF weather ensemble at each rain gauge, and evaporation from the Open-Meteo forecast. Thames flow at Farmoor is held at its last reading. Each rain member is run 4 times with the inflow error, 204 runs per works, so the chance carries both the weather's uncertainty and the model's own.

\[ P(\text{spill on forecast day } d) = \frac{\text{runs that spill that day}}{51 \text{ rain members} \times 4} \]
Unitchance in %, spill-hours in hours, spill volume in m³

2 · River water quality: E. coli and ammonia

The river page estimates what the river carries while a sewage spill upstream passes, every 100 m of river, for every day of the test year and the 7-day forecast. Each spilling works is mixed into the day's river flow and decays on the way downstream, on top of a background level. The source strengths and rates come from published studies; they are not fitted to local samples.

2.1

Which works are spilling that day

For past days, the works' own spill monitor. For forecast days, and past days with no usable monitor record, the physics model: a works counts as spilling if its chance is 50% or more.

\[ \text{spilling}_d = \begin{cases} \text{monitor recorded a spill} & \text{past days} \\ P(\text{spill}) \ge 0.5 & \text{forecast days} \end{cases} \]
Unityes or no, per works and day
2.2

How fast a works spills

Each works' typical spill rate in the physics model: its total modelled spill volume divided by its total modelled spill time over the test year. For works the model never spills, 0.37 × capacity, the typical ratio across works.

\[ r = \frac{\text{total spill volume (m}^3)}{\text{total spill-hours} \times 3600} \qquad \text{or} \qquad r = 0.37 \times \frac{\text{capacity (m}^3\text{/day)}}{86\,400} \]
Unitm³ per second
2.3

How much water each stretch carries

The river is cut into 100 m segments. On the Thames, Cherwell, Evenlode, Thame, Ock and Glyme, a segment takes the day's flow at the nearest EA gauge. Elsewhere it takes the gauges' median runoff per km² that day times its catchment area, estimated as 1.99 km² of land per km of river upstream (from the Glyme, Ock and Ewelme gauges). Flow is never below 0.005 m³/s. Forecast days hold the last reading.

\[ Q = \text{flow at the nearest gauge} \qquad \text{(gauged rivers)} \] \[ Q = \text{median runoff per km}^2 \times 1.99 \times \text{river length upstream (km)} \qquad \text{(elsewhere)} \]
Unitm³ per second
2.4

How long the sewage takes to arrive

The distance along the river from the works to the segment, at a steady 0.5 m/s, the speed at the high flows when spills happen. Segments more than 40 km downstream are not counted.

\[ t = \frac{\text{distance downstream (m)}}{0.5\ \text{m/s}} \]
Unithours (days in the decay formulas)
2.5

E. coli in the river

The spill's share of the water in the river, times the E. coli in storm sewage, shrinking as the bacteria die off on the way. They die off faster in summer sunlight. Every spilling works upstream adds its own share, on top of a background of 300 per 100 ml.

\[ E = 300 + \sum_{\text{spilling works upstream}} 1.5\times10^{6} \times \frac{r}{Q + r} \times e^{-k_E\, t} \] \[ k_E = \begin{cases} 0.5 \text{ per day} & \text{Oct–Mar} \\ 1.1 \text{ per day} & \text{Apr–Sep} \end{cases} \]
UnitE. coli per 100 ml (cfu/100 ml)
\(r/(Q+r)\) is the fraction of the river's water that is sewage. 1.5 million per 100 ml is the measured mean in storm overflows (Passerat et al. 2011); die-off rates from Servais et al. 2007.
2.6

Ammonia in the river

The same mixing, with ammonia in storm sewage (crude sewage diluted about 10 times by rainwater) and a slower loss as bacteria in the river turn it into nitrate. Background 0.04 mg/L, as measured in the Cherwell and the Thames.

\[ A = 0.04 + \sum_{\text{spilling works upstream}} 4 \times \frac{r}{Q + r} \times e^{-0.3\, t} \]
Unitmg of ammonia nitrogen per litre (mg N/L)
2.7

Advice when you hover a river

  • Swimming: E. coli up to 500 per 100 ml is safe, up to 1,000 means take care, above 1,000 is not safe (inland bathing-water standards: Excellent 500, Good 1,000).
  • Fish: ammonia up to 0.6 mg N/L is safe, up to 1.1 is stressful for sensitive fish, above 1.1 is harmful (Water Framework Directive standards for lowland rivers).
\[ \text{swimming: } E \le 500 \to \text{safe}, \quad E \le 1000 \to \text{take care}, \quad \text{above} \to \text{not safe} \] \[ \text{fish: } A \le 0.6 \to \text{safe}, \quad A \le 1.1 \to \text{stressful}, \quad \text{above} \to \text{harmful} \]
Unita label per segment and day
The standards judge a whole season of samples; here they are used as daily reference levels.

3 · The statistical backup

Used for overflows with no treated-flow data and for the next-day forecast. It learns each overflow's habits from its spill records.

3.1

Rain memory

A running total of recent rain that fades over \(\tau\) hours, with \(\tau\) chosen per overflow from 6, 12, 24, 48 or 96 hours. For the forecast it stops adding rain at midnight and only fades.

\[ F_t = e^{-1/\tau}\, F_{t-1} + \left(1 - e^{-1/\tau}\right)\tau\, R_t \]
Unitmm
3.2

Spill score and chance

A starting value, plus a weight on how high the Thames is at Farmoor, plus a weight on rain memory. The score is squashed into a chance between 0 and 100%, for each hour and for the whole day. The weights \(b\) and \(c\) are learned from past spill records.

\[ z_t = b_0 + b_1 \log Q^{\text{Farmoor}}_t + b_2 F_t \qquad P(\text{spill on day } d) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(c_0 + c_1 \max_{t \in d} z_t)}} \]
Unitchance in %

4 · The regulator watchlist

Two kinds of suspicious day are counted for each works, over Apr 2022 – Dec 2025 for the first and the held-out years for the second.

4.1

Spilling while not treating enough

A day it spilled for at least 20 hours while treating less than 80% of its permitted flow (FFT).

\[ \text{spill hours}_d \ge 20 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\text{TDV}_d}{86.4} < 0.8 \times \text{FFT} \]
Unitnumber of days (TDV in m³/day, FFT in litres/second)
4.2

A spill the weather doesn't explain

A day it spilled at least one hour, while both models gave it under a 5% chance.

\[ \text{spill hours}_d \ge 1 \quad \text{and} \quad P_{\text{physics}} < 0.05 \quad \text{and} \quad P_{\text{statistical}} < 0.05 \]
Unitnumber of days
4.3

Lead level

  • Strong: at least 10 all-day spill days with 30% or more under-treating, or 10 or more unexplained days.
  • Moderate: at least 5 under-treating days, or 3 or more unexplained days.
  • Low: otherwise. Not enough data: the works' flow data failed the audit.
Unita category

5 · How accuracy is scored

All scores use a year the models never saw while they were tuned (Oct 2025 – Oct 2026).

5.1

Brier score and skill

The average squared gap between the chance given and what happened (1 if it spilled, 0 if not); lower is better. Skill compares that with always guessing the usual spill rate: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better.

\[ \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{N}\sum (p - y)^2 \qquad \text{skill} = 1 - \frac{\text{Brier}}{\text{Brier}_{\text{usual rate}}} \]
Unitnone
5.2

AUC

How well the model ranks spill days above dry days, per works: 1 is perfect, 0.5 is no better than chance.

Unitnone

In one line

Rain (mm, measured or forecast), evaporation (mm), river flow (m³/s) and treated flow (m³/day) go into a water balance of each works, which gives a chance of a spill (%) and a spill volume (m³). The river model takes the days each works spills and its typical spill rate (m³/s), mixes them into every 100 m of river, and gives E. coli per 100 ml and ammonia in mg N/L while the spill passes, with advice for swimmers and fish.
